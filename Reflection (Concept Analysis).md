Working through the concept analysis shifted how I view software engineering—moving from writing deterministic instructions to orchestrating systems that reason, adapt, and act with varying degrees of autonomy. Contrasting traditional software with AI agents highlighted a clear engineering trade-off: deterministic code gives us predictability and straightforward verification, whereas agentic systems bring flexibility in open-ended environments at the cost of probabilistic behavior and harder-to-predict edge cases.

Connecting this to Agile practices and version control made practical sense. Because agent behavior depends heavily on prompts, model interactions, tool boundaries, and evolving requirements, rigid upfront plans rarely hold up well. Short feedback loops, iterative testing, and disciplined version control provide a structured way to inspect what actually happens in practice and refine the system safely.

Equally clear from this analysis is that autonomy without guardrails creates real risk. When an agent can trigger external actions or work with incomplete context, small errors can scale quickly, making security, privacy, governance, and meaningful human oversight central engineering responsibilities rather than optional extras.
